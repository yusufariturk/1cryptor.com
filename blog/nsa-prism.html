NSA PRISM: The Mass Surveillance Program That Never Ended — And What It Means for Your Cloud Files

2013 — 2024 Renewal 7 min read 5 sources
Active Surveillance Program
PRISM — the NSA's covert surveillance program giving US intelligence agencies access to data from Apple, Google, Microsoft, Facebook, and others — was revealed by Edward Snowden in June 2013. In April 2024, the US Congress renewed Section 702 of FISA (the legal basis for PRISM) for another 2 years, with expanded powers.

What PRISM Is and How It Works

PRISM is a covert data collection program operated by the United States National Security Agency (NSA) under Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA). First revealed by whistleblower Edward Snowden in June 2013, PRISM authorizes US intelligence agencies to collect internet communications and stored data from the servers of major American technology companies — without obtaining individual warrants.

The Snowden documents confirmed that the following companies participated in PRISM: Apple, Google, Microsoft, Facebook, Yahoo, Skype, YouTube, AOL, and PalTalk. Section 702 does not require individualized warrants. There is no judge reviewing specific targets or determining probable cause for each person whose data is collected.

2024: PRISM Gets Expanded Powers

In April 2024, the US Congress passed the Reforming Intelligence and Securing America Act — renewing Section 702 for an additional two years and significantly expanding its scope. Privacy advocates at the ACLU and EFF warned that the new language was broad enough to encompass cloud storage services, data centers, equipment custodians, and even WiFi hotspot operators.

The companies themselves cannot tell you whether they have received PRISM orders. The gag provisions of Section 702 prohibit disclosure. When Apple, Google, or Microsoft publish transparency reports, those reports omit PRISM-related compelled disclosures entirely, because Section 702 orders are classified.

Under Section 702, US intelligence agencies can access data held by US tech companies without a warrant targeted at the specific individual. The companies you trust with your files cannot tell you if they have received such an order.
